1. The Convoy Effect (Phase 2)

The Convoy Effect happens in FCFS when a long process takes the CPU
first and the short processes behind it have to wait a long time,
like small cars stuck behind a slow truck on a one-lane road.

In my run of cpu_scheduler.py, P1 needs 10 time units and arrives
first, so it runs from Time 00 to Time 10. P2 only needs 2 units
but waited 10 units. P3 only needs 3 units but waited 12 units.
The average waiting time under FCFS was 7.33 and the average
turnaround time was 12.33.

With Round Robin (Quantum = 3), P2 finished at Time 05 and P3
finished at Time 08, so the average waiting time dropped to 4.33
and the average turnaround time dropped to 9.33. The trade-off is
that P1 finished later at Time 15 and waited 5 units (0 in FCFS),
but the short processes were served much faster.

2. Choosing the Time Quantum

Very large quantum (e.g., 100): the quantum is larger than every
burst time, so each process finishes in its first time slice and is
never preempted. Round Robin then behaves exactly like FCFS. In my
run with Quantum = 100, P1, P2, and P3 finished at Time 10, 12, and
15, and the average waiting time was 7.33, the same as FCFS.

Very small quantum (e.g., 0.0001): the OS must preempt and switch
processes very often. Each context switch costs time because the
OS has to save and restore registers, the program counter, and
memory mappings. With a tiny quantum, the CPU spends most of its
time on switching instead of running the programs, so throughput
drops and the system becomes sluggish. Note that my simulator does
not count this overhead, so in my run with Quantum = 1 the average
waiting time was 4.33, the same as Quantum = 3, but a real OS would
be slower because of the many context switches.

3. AI Context

In ai_inference_scheduler.py, User_A_Essay needs 10 tokens and
User_B_Math needs 2 tokens. In FCFS mode, User_B has to wait until
User_A finishes the whole essay. If one token takes one step, User_B
gets its first token only at step 11 and finishes at step 12.

In Round Robin mode, the server generates one token per request in
turn (A, B, A, B, ...). User_B gets its first token at step 2 and
finishes at step 4, while User_A continues. In my output,
User_B_Math printed "finished early" before User_A_Essay.

Because every request gets one token per cycle, a long request
cannot monopolize the GPU, so short requests do not starve. This
lowers Time To First Token (TTFT) for interactive users, because
they see a response almost immediately instead of waiting for
someone else's long job to finish.
